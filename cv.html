Fifteen years, one page.

I've been a designer since 2011 and have yet to find a corner of it I don't like. Agency, textile retail, e-commerce, a SaaS startup, three years freelance — and now brand lead at a European online printer.

Brand, web, UX, print, photography, and the occasional detour into product ownership when the product needed it more than the marketing did. Everything below, in order, with the parts that actually mattered.

Worth mentioning

I came back from a trade fair with an uncomfortable observation: our B2B software solutions were genuinely good and almost nobody could tell from the way we talked about them. Nobody asked me to fix it, so I built the case anyway — a new website concept built around what the target group actually needed to know, positioning the products in language they use themselves. Strategy, UX, content structure and design in one piece of work.

The second thing I'm proud of here is quieter: I built AI image generation into the graphics workflow properly — briefing, generation, retouching, brand compliance, all of it documented so the team could actually use it. The result is that we no longer need a photo studio. Right now I'm pointing the same approach at brand development itself.

Context

Brand strategy and web design for smaller B2B companies and independent entrepreneurs — positioning, brand identity, marketing, and the website to carry it all.

What I did

  • Building and evolving brand identities — positioning, messaging, visual identity
  • Advising companies on brand presence, communication and digital presence, with consistency as the through-line
  • Websites as brand communication, not decoration: strategy, wireframes, build
  • UX concepts, then continuous optimisation against defined business and user goals
  • Running client projects and coordinating external freelancers and partners
  • And the rest of it: business development, sales, budgeting, project management, client care

Worth mentioning

The projects were good. The self-employment was better.

Three years running my own business taught me things no design job would have: how to hold a whole project in my head at once, how to price work, how to say no, and what a client actually means when they say "make it pop." I came out of it more flexible, more commercially minded and considerably harder to unsettle.

I marketed myself as a web designer, not a brand strategist — on purpose. Branding is abstract, and a lot of early-stage entrepreneurs don't yet know what to do with it or why they'd pay for it, so leading with it never landed with smaller clients. A website is concrete. Once I was actually talking to someone about their site, that's when I could make the case for the brand strategy underneath it — and by then they were usually convinced, because you can't design a website well without one.
